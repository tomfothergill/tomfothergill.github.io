Case study · 2021–2024

Ten thousand table tennis bets.

I built a betting model during lockdown and kept it running, with a few breaks, until 2024. This is how it worked, what it earned and why I stopped.

During lockdown I found myself watching table tennis in otherwise empty sports halls. There would be a player in red, another in blue and a referee who occasionally looked close to falling asleep. Some players appeared to be in their sixties, playing seven or eight matches a day. Once I had money on them, I sometimes looked them up online, which wasn't always reassuring.

Much of the usual sporting calendar had stopped, and these small Eastern European leagues had found an audience among people looking for something to bet on. This Forbes piece from June 2020 gives some of the background. What interested me was the volume of matches: thousands of players, fairly consistent conditions and a continuous supply of results and odds. It seemed a good place to try building a model.

Between March 2021 and April 2024, I placed @@bets@@ bets based on its recommendations. With the same stake on each bet, the log records a profit of @@profit_plain@@ units, or @@roi@@% of the total amount staked. The model won @@hit@@% of its bets, which takes a little explaining.

Choosing a price worth taking

I used Elo, the rating system familiar from chess, to estimate each player's chance of winning. Each player started with a rating of 1,500. After a match, the winner gained rating points and the loser gave them up, with a larger adjustment when the result was unexpected. I used a K-factor of 10 to control those adjustments, updated after whole matches and shared the ratings across leagues.

The difference between two players' ratings gave me a win probability, which I converted into odds. I could then compare my price with the bookmaker's and look for a large enough difference to justify a bet. A player could be unlikely to win and still be worth backing if the payout was high enough for the chance I thought they had.

I also chose to concentrate on outsiders. For most of the project, a minimum-odds filter excluded anything below 3.00, although I lowered it at times to experiment with shorter prices. That was an explicit part of the selection process, so the high proportion of long-priced bets in the results largely reflects how I configured the system.

The daily routine ran on PythonAnywhere. It took in the latest results, updated the ratings, priced the next day's fixtures and emailed me the bets that passed the filters. I placed the bets, logged the results and made adjustments as the project went along.

The average odds taken were @@mean@@. With decimal odds, a winning bet at 4.00 returns four units including the stake, so it covers three one-unit losses. The actual prices varied, but that is how a system winning fewer than one bet in three could finish ahead.

Where the returns came from

The overall return hides some substantial differences. Setka Cup supplied 5,773 bets and 776.19 units of profit, making it the largest contributor. TT Cup Ukraine and the Russian Liga Pro also performed well. The same approach was roughly break-even on the women's Setka Cup and lost money on Czech Liga Pro. I didn't investigate those differences at the time, so I can't offer an explanation for them now.

The odds breakdown is revealing too. Bets from 5.00 up to, but not including, 8.00 returned 25.48% across 1,346 selections. At 8.00 and above, just two of 94 bets won, losing 77 units. Longer odds clearly weren't enough on their own.

I had suspected that punters' preference for favourites might leave value in outsiders. These results describe the bets I selected, but they don't establish why the prices were available. They also can't show what would have happened with a different minimum-odds filter, because the log only contains the bets I placed.

Watching the matches raised other questions. A player could start at 3.00, win the first game and then drift to 5.00 or 6.00. It sometimes felt as though other people betting knew more about the eventual result than the scoreline could tell them. Players' apparent levels of effort varied as well, and a rating based on past results couldn't directly account for how interested someone was in their eighth match of the day.

A smaller market to work with

Unfortunately, the range of leagues I was betting on narrowed around the time Russia invaded Ukraine. My final TT Cup Ukraine bet was two days before the invasion. Bets on the Russian Liga Pro stopped within a fortnight, and Czech Liga Pro followed a few weeks later. TT Cup appeared in the log in April, but most of my bets were now on Setka Cup.

The change shows up in both the number of bets and the return. From March 2021 through February 2022, I placed 7,168 bets for a 13.47% return. Over the rest of 2022, I placed 1,849 bets and returned 5.16%. By December, keeping it going no longer felt worth the effort.

I restarted in October 2023 because I fancied giving it another go. The daily process was easy to switch back on, and the return recovered: the final 1,365 bets earned 160.42 units, or 11.75%. There were still fewer matches to bet on, with almost all the selections on Setka Cup. Eventually, the bookmakers limited my accounts to penny stakes, bringing the project to an end in April 2024.

What I can take from it

I ended up with a fairly simple model that made money over a substantial number of bets. I also spent enough time running it to see how much the result depended on the markets available and the bets I could actually place. A return of around 12% was less useful when there were fewer opportunities, and penny stakes made it impractical to continue.

Having the full log makes this an unusually satisfying project to revisit. I can follow the profit through individual bets, separate the leagues and compare different parts of the run. It records the results of a system I adjusted over time; it isn't a test of one unchanged model. There are still things I would like to understand, particularly the differences between leagues, but the record gives me somewhere concrete to start.
