# On the avoidance of slop

I’ve spent quite a lot of time recently choosing ornamental patterns for this website. This wasn’t something I expected to become particularly interested in when I started building my own personal portfolio site, but I have now developed opinions about nineteenth-century ornament and the appropriate weight of a decorative navy border. AI has made the process of building things so much more straightforward that I’m finding I have more time to spend thinking about these details. I had probably expected it to mean spending less time on a website. Instead, I seem to be spending that time differently.

The first version was purple and tangerine. I still quite like that combination, and a personal portfolio site seemed a reasonable place to indulge it. Realistically, I’m likely to spend more time tinkering with this site than anyone else spends reading it, so enjoying the process is a fairly substantial part of the point. The articles had purple backgrounds, with the text in a column down the middle and a lot of empty space on either side. I started wondering whether I could put something interesting in those margins. Initially I was thinking about swirls, then Indian-style patterning, and searching for references led me towards floral and more elaborate ornamental designs that I found myself particularly drawn to.

That small idea ended up changing the whole site. It also helped me put a finger on something I’ve been thinking about more generally: how to use AI to make things that feel considered, when it is so easy to produce something that merely looks finished.

There are plenty of recognisable habits in AI writing. The “it’s not X, it’s Y” construction is one. There are the familiar words such as “delve” and “tapestry”, and the tendency for quite ordinary things to be described as “quietly” doing something significant. People have assembled [whole catalogues of these patterns](https://tropes.fyi/directory?category=word-choice). There are visual equivalents, too: components that start to feel familiar because you keep being offered versions of them. One I’ve been given is a rounded, pill-like box with a coloured cap at one end, lending it a slightly odd sense of depth. There’s nothing inherently wrong with it, but once you start recognising these recurring choices, you notice how often they appear without much connection to the thing being built.

I don’t think avoiding slop can amount to maintaining a list of forbidden words and components, though. Those habits will change, and there are good uses for all of them. You can remove every recognisable phrase from a piece of writing and still be left with something that says very little. Equally, a website can avoid every fashionable AI design cliché and still feel like a collection of unrelated decisions. Knowing what you dislike is useful, but it only gets you part of the way towards deciding what you want. I’m aware that, on a larger screen, these observations are surrounded by little Egyptian columns, which invites some scrutiny of my own judgement. They were originally much bigger, which I’m choosing to count as evidence of restraint.

Building my own site has been an interesting way to explore this because I had so little to start from. At work, there is a company identity: colours, fonts, assets and some understanding of what belongs. For a personal website, I could choose almost anything. That sounds freeing, but it also means there is very little against which to judge the first thing the model produces. My initial response was partly reactive. I wanted to get away from what I’d started to think of as the Claudeification of everything: the papery backgrounds, restrained layouts and tasteful typography that seemed to turn up so often in the designs I was seeing. Purple and tangerine felt like a fairly decisive way of doing that.

There is an obvious irony here, given that you’re reading this on a softly coloured page in a serif font. I can see why those choices recur in Claude’s output: they look good, and they make for a pleasant place to read. I ended up wanting some of those qualities myself. The original version was distinctive, and I liked that it was a little excessive, but once I started thinking about ornamental borders, I realised I was interested in a different character for the site. Adding flowers to the purple pages would have left me trying to reconcile two ideas that hadn’t really been chosen together.

Following the new idea meant reconsidering the rest of the design. The backgrounds became softer, the typography more bookish, and the pages started to suggest printed documents. The homepage became a cream panel framed by ornament, with something of the feel of an old book’s title page. Those choices gave the ornament somewhere to belong, and the ornament gave the quieter parts of the design a context. I was arriving at some familiar visual ingredients, but I now had reasons for choosing them and a clearer sense of how they should work together.

One of the most useful things I did was look outside the AI tools for the material itself. I started finding illustrations and ornamental designs in scans of old books, including the collections on [Old Book Illustrations](https://www.oldbookillustrations.com/). There were things there I would never have thought to describe in a prompt, let alone ask a model to invent. Botanical patterns were the starting point, but the appeal extends to ornament more generally: borders, geometric arrangements, decorative fragments and all sorts of details originally made for quite different purposes.

I like the idea of treating these as found objects. A particular arrangement of shapes or an unusual border gives you something concrete to respond to. You can like one part of it, dislike another, or notice a colour combination you hadn’t considered. It brings decisions into the process that weren’t generated from the same conversation as everything else. It also gives you the pleasure of finding something, which I’ve discovered is a fairly effective way of becoming invested in how it is used.

There was still plenty of work involved in making those objects fit. One image contained several bands of decoration, and I only wanted the central vine. Another had a powdery blue background that looked promising in isolation but felt wrong alongside the article. I asked AI to make two versions so I could compare them: one with the pattern recoloured to match the cream page, and another with the page changed to match the blue pattern. I preferred the cream, then decided that the dark framing bands were too heavy and asked for those to be removed as well. Finding an image I liked was the beginning of that process; I still needed to see what happened when it became part of an actual page.

These are small decisions, but they are also quite specific decisions. “Make it more tasteful” would have been a much less useful instruction. Seeing two versions next to each other let me work out what I actually liked, including things I couldn’t have articulated before looking at them. This is where the time saved on implementation becomes useful. With AI taking care of the bulk of the building, I can afford to try a few alternatives and pay attention to the differences. The interesting decisions get more of my time, even when they concern a fairly narrow strip down the side of a page.

Using an old illustration doesn’t automatically make a design good, of course. You can assemble a perfectly incoherent page out of beautiful source material. What helped here was settling on a shared idea for how the material would be used. Each article could have its own pattern and colours while keeping the same typography, reading width and treatment of the margins. The homepage could be more decorative and still feel related. Even the charts and diagrams needed to belong to the page around them. Otherwise, you scroll past a carefully chosen border and suddenly arrive at a figure that looks as though it has been pasted in from somebody else’s dashboard.

That is something I notice frequently in AI-built interfaces. Individual sections can look entirely reasonable, but the relationship between them is weak. A new feature arrives with its own visual habits, and another arrives with a slightly different set. Each may have been reviewed and approved in isolation, without much attention to the accumulated result. Looking at the whole page becomes an important part of the work, particularly after several rounds of changes. The same attention is needed with the words, where a long conversation can leave behind traces that are less immediately visible than a mismatched chart.

A failure I keep encountering happens when I ask a model to remove something. Suppose it has added a section about a topic I don’t want to cover. I explain that, and the next version contains a sentence telling the reader that this topic is outside the scope of the article. From my side of the conversation, I can see what has happened: the response acknowledges my correction. From the reader’s side, it is a strange thing to encounter. They weren’t there when the section was suggested, and they have no particular reason to wonder where it went. The final page has acquired a little piece of the editing conversation.

I find this more revealing than an overused adjective. The model has written something that makes sense as a response to me, and that response has ended up in material intended for somebody else. It can happen in articles, but also in interface labels, explanatory text and footers. A sentence survives because it reassures the person giving instructions that an instruction has been followed. Keeping track of what the eventual reader knows, and what they have any reason to care about, seems to be something I need to supervise quite carefully.

I’ve had occasions where I haven’t done that carefully enough. I’ve sat in meetings discussing a document I produced with AI’s help and felt embarrassed by some very small detail I’d allowed through: a heading that didn’t quite make sense, a sentence I wouldn’t really stand by, or a visual motif that didn’t fit. It’s an uncomfortable way to discover that reviewing something in a chat and putting it in front of other people are quite different experiences. A detail that seemed harmless while I was making changes can become surprisingly conspicuous when somebody else is looking at it.

I try to catch these by reading the result as someone arriving without any of that history. Does this sentence explain something they need to understand? Does this label help them use the page? Would any of this be puzzling if they hadn’t seen the brief? Sometimes an explanation of scope is useful. Often, though, the right edit is simply to delete the thing and let the remaining content make sense on its own. I want to be comfortable with the words and visual choices that remain, including the small ones.

This article has been written that way, too. I talked through the ideas with AI, gave it the story of building the site, and asked it to help turn a fairly sprawling collection of thoughts into a draft. I then went through it paragraph by paragraph, keeping things I liked, rejecting things that didn’t sound like me and adding details it couldn’t have known. Some of the wording is mine and quite a lot was proposed by the model. This is increasingly how I work, and writing about avoiding slop seems a reasonable occasion to be explicit about it. I still need to be happy putting my name to the result.

What I’ve enjoyed about building this site is that AI has made this sort of attention easier to act on. I can follow an idea, see it on the page, change my mind and try again without having to implement every detail by hand. The process still needs judgement, but there is much more room to exercise it when every experiment doesn’t come with a substantial amount of work. I’m increasingly interested in using that freedom to spend longer on the parts I care about.

I still have some affection for the purple and tangerine version. The current site feels much more like somewhere I’d want to spend time, though, and I can explain how it got that way. Whether choosing ornamental borders was the best use of an afternoon is a separate question, but it was a very enjoyable one.
